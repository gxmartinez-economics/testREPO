2  Living Standards and Productivity

Imagine living in your hometown, today, with only $10-$20/week to pay for rent, groceries, gas, food, education, clothing, medical care, and fun. That was the situation of the vast majority of humanity since the dawn of human history until relatively recently. In other words, everyone was not only poor, but miserably poor. Even the kings and queens didn’t have electricity, toilets, or the internet. Millions died from diseases that today we cure with soap and water.

This might be one of the most amazing graphs ever. For millennia, living standards barely changed. Then, somethings switched, and living standards have grown exponentially, for 200 years, in practically every country in the world.

Figure 2.1 shows GDP per capita, the average value of goods and services available to a person in the country (rather than GDP, the total size of the economy). Per capita is what we actually care about, because it determines our material living standards. Here’s a great comparison between the UK, the USA, and China.

England’s income per person grew at an average of 0.05 percent per year between 1250 and 1650, implying that GDP per capita would double every 1400 years. From 1650 to 1820, the average annual growth rate rose to 0.41 percent, reducing doubling time to only (!) 170 years. China’s growth rate between 1150 and 1968 was a negative 0.00031 percent, essentially flat for 800 years.

Starting in the early 1820s, something happened. Table 2.1 Western offshoots like the United States were growing faster than everyone else, at triple that “higher” English rate of the previous period, and sustained it. All other regions matched that growth rate after World War I, and especially after 1950. By 1980, you were disappointed if your country wasn’t growing at 2 percent. The world-average growth rate today is triple the nineteenth-century average. Instead of taking a lifetime for incomes to double, it only takes a generation. China’s real GDP per capita today is just a little over $19,000.

What is the explanation for these extraordinary patterns? How does an economy grow over time?

2.1 How does economic growth work?

You are the ruler of your kingdom. The typical person enjoys approximately $1000/year (about $2.75/day) of goods and services. GDP per capita grows at about 0.1 percent a year, which means that it will double in approximately 693 years. Then, it starts growing at 1 percent a year, and now it doubles in 70 years.

You are the president of your country, where GDP per capita is $20,000. You are going to implement a policy that will increase the growth rate of real GDP per person from 2 to 4 percent. Compare the value of real GDP per person twenty years from now under each policy. (For context, between 1978 and 2011, US living standards grew at an annual average of 1.81 percent; Chinese living standards grew at an annual average of 8.8 percent.)

How do we calculate the number of years for real GDP per capita to double? Suppose \(y\) starts at \(y_0\): for example, let’s say that real GDP per capita starts at $20,000. The value of \(y\) after one year, if it’s growing at \(r\), is \(y_1=y_0(1+r)\). After two years, it would be \(y_2=y_0(1+r)^2\). The value of \(y\) after \(t\) years, if it’s growing at \(r\), it will be \(y_t = y_0(1+r)^t\). When will \(y_t=2\times y_0\)? A little math will help. It turns out doubling time \(t\) is approximately \[t=\frac{72}{r\times100}\]so it is called the Rule of 72. Try it: how long will it take for your savings to double if they are growing at 6 percent?

  2. OECD (2025) “OECD job markets remain resilient but population ageing will cause significant labour shortages and fiscal pressures.” How could population aging slow growth in output per person, and which responses work through employment rather than productivity?

  3. Atsushi Iimi (2006) Botswana: Avoiding the Resource Curse. What besides diamonds helps explain Botswana’s success? Identify one limitation the author still acknowledges.

  4. Diana Roy and Amelia Cheatham (2024) “Venezuela: The Rise and Fall of a Petrostate”. How could oil wealth weaken a country’s ability to produce other goods and services—and weaken the incentives for accountable government?

Material living standards concern access to goods and services. Is that the same as being happy, holy, or good? No, but sometimes we are interested in something basic like material productive capacity. In this chapter, we are interested in “price-adjusted output” also called “real GDP,” which we label with \(Y\). As a matter of fact, we care about the amount of goods and services available to people in a country, \(Y\), divided by how many people there are in the country, \(N\): \[ \frac{Y}{N} \] which we call Real GDP per capita, or real GDP per person.

2.3 Adam Smith’s Explanation

In 1776, a professor in Scotland published a book that would change the world. Adam Smith’s Wealth of Nations is not the only famous document that came out that year, but it’s pretty famous. He opens the book, basically, by giving us the key to the question of this chapter: why do some countries grow richer than others? His answer boils it down to two factors: (1) the effectiveness of labor, by which we mean output per worker, also known as average labor productivity, or real GDP divided by the number of people employed, \(Y/E\), and (2) the proportion engaged in useful labor, which is the employment-to-population ratio, \(E\) divided by \(N\).

Let’s do a little math. Divide both sides of the real-GDP-per-capita fraction by \(E\). Because we are dividing both sides by the same number, it is still the old \(Y/N\) in new clothes. Then rearrange, which basically means we have “decomposed” real GDP per capita into its constituent parts. \[ \frac{Y / E}{N / E} = \frac{Y}{E}\times \frac{E}{N} \] Just to keep the notation compact, let’s say that \(y\) is real GDP per person, \(ALP\) is average labor productivity, and \(EMR\) is the employment-population ratio. Then we can say \[ y=ALP \times EPR \tag{2.1}\]

2.3.1 Alternative Explanations: some wrong turns

Smith’s was not the first theory of riches-versus-poverty. Often people think that having a lot of natural resources is the key to riches. More resources means that you have more with which to produce, so you can make more GDP for each person.

But as the readings suggest, it’s not about the resources you have, but about what you do with them. There’s such a thing as having too much unearned wealth. Resource rents can be captured by kings, war lords, and corporate titans, easy streams of public revenue can be mismanaged, and dependence creates vulnerability.

In Figure 2.2 you can see that the countries with the most natural resources rents are not necessarily very rich: in fact, some of the world’s richest countries earn only a small fraction of their income out of natural resources. Hover over the following graph to identify each country. The bubble identifies the size of the population; the colors identify continents.

Really? How do resource-poor countries get around their scarcity? They trade. Long ago, countries figured out that they could get access to other country’s resources by producing what those countries needed, in exchange for the resources they lacked. For example, country P could by the raw materials from country R, manufacture them into something desirable, and sell it back to country R.

So far, that’s true. But then history took a wrong turn by thinking that it was all about trade: specifically, a trade surplus. Mercantilists thought that the wealth of nations is the accumulation of gold and silver, and that to accumulate precious metals you need more exports than imports: favorable trade balances became central national objectives; trade deficits were thought to be the source and symptom of national weakness.

To make trade deficits go away, countries used tariffs to make foreign goods more expensive. But you can see in Figure 2.3 that, if anything, richer countries have lower average tariffs.

Tariffs protect domestic producers from foreign competition, and domestic consumers pay the higher price. So mercantilism is an attractive policy to kings, warlords, and corporate titans, but not the key to long term economic growth.

In short: neither abundant natural resources nor high tariffs explain differences in living standards.

2.3.2 Back to Smith’s explanations

Let’s go back to Adam Smith’s two explanations for economic prosperity: average labor productivity and the employment-to-population ratio. What could cause \(EPR\) to rise?

  • Fewer people go to school. Rather than going to college, people start to work at four, raising the proportion of workers in the population.

  • People retire later, or live shorter lives, so that they spend fewer years in retirement.

  • There are more young people working.

It’s OK if you think that there must be something wrong with this explanation. Doesn’t “high living standards” mean the ability to retire early? Doesn’t more education correlate with higher income?

Do you see any patterns in Figure 2.4? The countries with the highest \(EPR\) are countries that are quite poor. It makes sense: if you are very poor, you can’t afford to retire and you can’t afford to go to school. But not going to school makes you poor: the lowest \(EPR\) is in countries that are very poor. In the middle, you have very rich countries and very poor countries: USA and Congo have approximately the same \(EPR\). It would seem that \(EPR\) is not a very good predictor of \(y\). Do you have an alternative theory?

2.4 Average Labor Productivity as the Key Determinant of Living Standards

The single most important determinant of long-term living standards is average labor productivity.

Figure 2.5 shows you the incredibly strong correlation between worker productivity and living standards.

Figure 2.6 shows you three related, and very important, facts. First, the productivity of labor has been increasing, by a lot, in nearly every country. Second, workers in some countries are much more productive than in others, and their productivity grows at radically different rates. China’s average worker produced $0.50/hour in 1952 and today produces $17.7/hour; in South Korea, productivity has gone from $1.70/hour to $53.6/hour. The rate of productivity increase is nothing short of fantastic for some countries … but pretty pedestrian for others.

And third, you can see that, although productivity is key, it is not the only explanation: German workers are nearly as productive as American workers, but German real GDP per capita is only 80% of America’s: the difference is due to Germany’s smaller employment-to-population ratio.
